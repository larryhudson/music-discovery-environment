# After Josaleigh Pollett’s *If I Let It Quiet*

**Best next album: Loma’s *Don’t Shy Away*.** It most closely extends this particular record’s combination of intimate singing, environmental sound, electronic movement, and relationships sustained across distance.

This shortlist follows the album’s **emotional architecture and production**, not a generic “female indie folk” category. Descriptions below are based on artist commentary, credits, and published criticism—not firsthand audio auditioning.

## 1. What the seed actually is

### Verified identity and listening signal

The spelling is **Josaleigh Pollett — *If I Let It Quiet***. Both [Bandcamp](https://josaleighpollett.bandcamp.com/album/if-i-let-it-quiet) and the Qobuz catalog confirm **July 24, 2026**, twelve tracks. It is an **Audio Antihero/Lavender Vinyl** release.

The supplied history shows:

- **188 album plays across 17 distinct days**
- **203 Pollett plays across 20 days**
- Every seed track appears at least thirteen times.
- “The Witness” leads with 20 plays, followed by “Bed of Quiet” with 19 and “Like a River” with 18.

That is sustained attachment, not one exploratory traversal. The neighboring repeat signals—BABEHOVEN, 123 plays/nine days; Johanna Samuels, 70/nine; Hana Stretton, 74/six; Squirrel Flower, 46/six; Westside Cowboy, 112/twelve—support retaining **songcraft and emotional immediacy**, even when taking the production somewhere stranger.

### Emotional and lyrical dimensions

Pollett’s own [track-by-track commentary](https://goldflakepaint.ghost.io/track-by-track-guide-josaleigh-pollett-if-i-let-it-quiet/) makes several distinctions that generic similarity recommendations would miss:

- **Connection without permanence:** “The Witness” asks to be recognized while changing, rather than loved only as an established version of oneself.
- **Family is not automatically nourishment:** “Cherry” concerns distance from Pollett’s brother and the expectations placed on blood relationships.
- **Quiet can be frightening:** “Like a River” emerged from the unexpectedly noisy inner life accompanying sobriety. Its central question—“Who am I if not my thinking?”—makes stillness an identity problem.
- **Memory is unstable, not merely comforting:** “Radio Player” interweaves childhood memory with *Poltergeist*.
- **The ending accepts transformation:** “Tacit” concerns allowing the creative partnership to change instead of preserving it so tightly that it dies.

### Voice and production

This is a jointly constructed world. Pollett and **Jordan Watko/Crowd Shy** wrote and performed the record between Salt Lake City and Osaka; Watko produced it; **Andrew Goldring** supplied mixing, mastering, additional production, and instrumental contributions.

The organic/electronic relationship is unusually literal:

- Backyard footsteps, an out-of-tune shed piano, and an airplane become the opening soundscape.
- Accidental microphone contact becomes percussion in “Like a River.”
- Japanese street celebrations and family voices enter “Yasaka, Natsu.”
- Pollett’s dog Townes remains present through recorded snores and other domestic sounds.
- “Bed of Quiet” incorporates material recorded by **Chris Walla and Beau Sorenson**.
- Goldring’s backing vocal on “Cherry” was intended as a gentle, brotherly connection.
- A discarded, belted version of “The Witness” survives inside its final production as an alternative emotional timeline.

[Pollett’s Atwood interview](https://atwoodmagazine.com/jptw-josaleigh-pollett-the-witness-if-i-let-it-quiet-feature-music-interview/) explicitly identifies **Low, Sharon Van Etten, and Jenn Wasner** as production reference points. [Treble’s review](https://www.treblezine.com/josaleigh-pollett-if-i-let-it-quiet-review/) describes breakbeats, reversed piano, distant cello, synth arpeggios, and roaring guitar feedback beneath the gentle vocal.

**The recommendation target:** songs whose human center remains audible while their surroundings become unstable, tactile, and emotionally meaningful.

## 2. Ranked recommendations

### 1. Loma — *Don’t Shy Away*
**Released October 23, 2020 · Sub Pop**

**Entry track: “Ocotillo.”** Then hear “Homing” before committing to the complete album.

**Why this follows the seed:** Loma builds songs from the blurred boundary between a physical environment and an electronic one. Published descriptions identify insects, creaking doors, bird calls, synths, woodwinds, and percussion as parts of one inhabited landscape—not decorative atmosphere. That directly follows Pollett and Watko turning their separate homes into musical material.

Emily Cross’s airy voice provides a human guide through mechanical churn and obscured instrumental detail. The themes of solitude, impermanence, and finding light in darkness also fit the seed better than uncomplicated pastoral calm.

There is a particularly strong connection in **“Homing”**: Brian Eno developed it from emailed stems, and its treatment of presence across geographical distance mirrors the seed’s transcontinental collaboration.

**Network connection:** Jenn Wasner—named by Pollett as an influence—contributes piano and bass. This is an influence/collaborator-network connection, not a shared seed producer.

**Caveat:** More enigmatic and restrained than Pollett’s sharply personal writing; less of the seed’s overt guitar release.

**Confidence: high.**

- [Listen on Bandcamp; release details and credits](https://lomamusic.bandcamp.com/album/dont-shy-away)
- [Qobuz](https://open.qobuz.com/album/urvc6q94muphi)
- [Pitchfork review](https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/loma-dont-shy-away/)

### 2. Beth Orton — *Kidsticks*
**Released May 27, 2016 · Anti-**

**Entry track: “Flesh and Blood.”**

**Why this follows the seed:** The important resemblance is an emotionally legible, lived-in voice inside adventurous electronics. Orton and co-producer **Andrew Hung of Fuck Buttons** surround songs with unusual rhythmic patterns, synth movement, substantial bass, and live instrumental contributions.

The [Pitchfork review](https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/21917-kidsticks/) specifically describes vocal cracks and quavers, changing vocal registers, and imagery involving weather, bodies, memory, and loss. [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2016/05/19/477927199/first-listen-beth-orton-kidsticks) emphasizes searching and identity—including losing sleep and the same moon connecting separate people.

“Flesh and Blood” is the best doorway because its repeated acceptance of “whatever this is” offers a useful continuation of “Tacit”: uncertainty becoming something one can inhabit rather than solve.

**Connection strength:** Treble independently names *Kidsticks* as a comparable album. The recommendation is therefore supported both by album-specific research and an explicit seed review.

**Caveat:** Often brighter, more playful, and less haunted than *If I Let It Quiet*. This is not simply another acoustic Beth Orton record.

**Confidence: high.**

- [Qobuz](https://open.qobuz.com/album/t2n1pb9lr2q6a)
- [Pitchfork review](https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/21917-kidsticks/)
- [NPR review](https://www.npr.org/2016/05/19/477927199/first-listen-beth-orton-kidsticks)

### 3. Sparklehorse — *It’s a Wonderful Life*
**Released 2001 · Qobuz edition dated July 3 · Capitol**

**Entry track: “Apple Bed.”**

**Why this follows the seed:** This is the strongest older detour for **domestic unease, bodily imagery, fragile delivery, and damaged sonic surfaces**. Mark Linkous’s songs combine murmured vocals with old keyboards, static, subtle electronics, and occasional abrasive guitar.

The [contemporary Pitchfork review](https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/7398-its-a-wonderful-life/) describes a mixture of lo-fi and hi-fi aesthetics, humming keyboards, surreal animal imagery, and electronics supporting fully developed songs. That is a defensible continuation of the seed’s ghosts, dogs, nourishment imagery, and acoustic songs transformed by production.

“Apple Bed” supplies a quietly unsettling introduction; “Piano Fire” explores the louder guitar branch, while “Babies on the Sun” develops the collage/electronic branch.

**Production connection:** Dave Fridmann contributes to this record’s sonic character. This is an aesthetic parallel, not a documented personnel connection to Pollett.

**Caveat:** More surreal, death-shadowed, and oblique. “Dog Door,” featuring Tom Waits, is a conspicuously harsher interruption. Qobuz’s fourteen-track edition contains material beyond the twelve-track listing reproduced in some contemporary reviews.

**Confidence: medium-high.**

- [Qobuz](https://open.qobuz.com/album/arxc79n643kpb)
- [Pitchfork review](https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/7398-its-a-wonderful-life/)
- [RTÉ review](https://www.rte.ie/entertainment/music-reviews/2001/0628/448473-sparklehorse/)

### 4. phoneswithchords — *piecemeal*
**Released May 2, 2025 · Amiable Alleycat Music**

**Entry track: “put pressure feat. Josaleigh Pollett.”**

**Why this follows the seed:** This is the direct collaborator route, but it earns inclusion through its writing rather than merely featuring Pollett. The published lyrics repeatedly return to old wounds, emotional fortification, borrowed shame, nightmares, waiting, and unresolved forgiveness.

Those themes extend “Bed of Quiet” and “Repeating”: defenses that preserve pain, thoughts that become grooves, and the difficulty of recognizing one’s own patterns.

The artist describes the album as sad, slow songs and slowcore. Credits verify **Nick Webber’s mixing/mastering**, synth contributions, layered harmonies, and cardboard percussion on “put pressure”—a modest but meaningful tactile-production parallel.

**Caveat:** Evidence is strongest for lyrics and credits, not detailed independent sonic criticism. Expect the slow-song side of the seed rather than its expansive electronic peaks. The page retains old “work in progress” language despite displaying a release date and twelve-track album.

**Confidence: medium-high for emotional fit; medium for whole-album sonic fit.**

- [Bandcamp album, lyrics, date, and credits](https://phoneswithchords.bandcamp.com/album/piecemeal)
- [Direct entry-track page and guest-vocal credit](https://phoneswithchords.bandcamp.com/track/put-pressure-feat-josaleigh-pollett)

### 5. Low — *Double Negative*
**Released September 14, 2018 · Sub Pop**

**Entry track: “Always Trying to Work It Out.”**

**Why this follows the seed:** Take the friction between clear singing and distorted surroundings in “Bed of Quiet,” or the feedback in “We All Got It Right,” and move it to the foreground.

With producer **BJ Burton**, Low makes noise, interruption, and damaged vocal transmission integral to composition. The [Pitchfork review](https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/low-double-negative/) describes performer and producer working from sketches together, rather than simply distressing finished songs afterward. That parallels Pollett’s account of finally hearing something jointly written and explored, not “a Josaleigh song produced by Jordan.”

Its anxiety about being heard through interference offers a darker answer to “The Witness.”

**Direct influence:** Pollett names Low in the Atwood interview. I found no statement identifying *Double Negative* specifically as the seed’s model.

**Caveat:** The most demanding recommendation here. Lyrics can become unintelligible; its emotional temperature is much grimmer than the seed’s sustaining friendship.

**Confidence: high for production relevance; medium for immediate enjoyment.**

- [Qobuz](https://open.qobuz.com/album/onnegh3k8uqe5)
- [Pitchfork review](https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/low-double-negative/)

### 6. Juana Molina — *Un Día*
**Released 2008 · Domino; verified Qobuz edition dated October 6**

**Entry track: “Los Hongos de Marosa.”**

**Why this follows the seed:** This is the unexpected pick for the listener drawn to **“Like a River,” “Repeating,” and the instrumental interludes**. Molina treats guitar, electronic processing, and voice as material that can circulate, overlap, and change function.

[Pitchfork](https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/12297-un-dia/) describes circular acoustic guitar, electronics, rumbling drones, and vocals acting as a breathy thread through polyrhythms. The [BBC review](https://www.bbc.co.uk/music/reviews/xzvr/) emphasizes warm, relatively lo-fi electronics and voices reshaped into humming, chanting, and nonverbal loops.

This follows the seed’s **organic origins of synthetic sound**, rather than its particular lyrical subjects.

**Caveat:** Spanish lyrics and substantial nonverbal singing make this a weaker continuation of Pollett’s English-language narrative detail. Longer, more hypnotic structures reward attention to sound rather than immediate chorus payoff.

**Confidence: medium-high for production exploration; medium for emotional continuity.**

- [Qobuz](https://open.qobuz.com/album/5034202022756)
- [Pitchfork review](https://pitchfork.com/reviews/albums/12297-un-dia/)
- [BBC review and track listing](https://www.bbc.co.uk/music/reviews/xzvr/)

## 4. Recommended listening route

**For three full albums:**  
**Loma → Beth Orton → Sparklehorse**

That moves from the closest environmental/emotional continuation, through more animated electronic songcraft, into an older, haunted production lineage.

Then branch according to what mattered most:

- **Words, wounds, and Pollett’s voice:** phoneswithchords.
- **Clear songs struggling through interference:** Low.
- **Loops, tactile rhythm, and organic/electronic transformation:** Juana Molina.

A quick entry-track route is:

**“Ocotillo” → “Flesh and Blood” → “Apple Bed” → “put pressure” → “Always Trying to Work It Out” → “Los Hongos de Marosa.”**

Release metadata and tracks were checked against Bandcamp and/or Qobuz catalog records; Qobuz links use catalog-returned album IDs.
